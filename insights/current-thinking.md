Current Thinking

A space for thinking with the present.

This is where I reflect on current developments, questions and conversations around climate change, biodiversity, environmental governance and landscapes.

These are not formal publications or finished arguments. They are working reflections—ideas that I am exploring, questions that remain open, and connections I am beginning to see between current events and my research.

I am particularly interested in what happens when global environmental challenges meet particular landscapes, institutions and communities: how policies are translated into practice, how different forms of knowledge interact, and how people negotiate change.

Some reflections may begin with a current event, a new report, a conversation, a field experience, or simply a question that I cannot stop thinking about.

## When Carbon Becomes the Currency of the Landscape

Carbon finance is increasingly presented as a way to make forests and other ecosystems financially valuable enough to protect. But does putting a price on carbon actually protect landscapes? Or does it simply add another layer of actors, rules, and financial relationships to already complex governance systems?

### What prompted this thinking?

This question came to me while looking through recent vacancies on LinkedIn. I began noticing an increasing number of positions related to carbon finance, carbon markets and climate-related investment. It made me wonder about the growing role of carbon not only as an environmental concern, but also as a financial and professional sector.

At around the same time, I came across a discussion shared by Novara Media on Instagram, which prompted me to think more critically about some of the assumptions behind carbon markets and carbon finance.

Together, these observations made me pause.

### What am I thinking about?

What happens to a landscape when carbon becomes something that can be measured, traded and financed?

My starting point is not to argue that carbon finance is inherently good or bad. Rather, I want to think through what happens when carbon finance enters landscapes that are already governed by multiple institutions, actors, rules and forms of knowledge.

### A question I am carrying forward

Does carbon finance actually contribute to reducing deforestation and supporting conservation?

Where does the money go?

Who receives it, and who decides how it is used?

Does carbon finance strengthen the institutions and communities already managing landscapes, or does it create another governance layer alongside existing conservation, forestry, development and customary institutions?

And perhaps a broader question:

If money is already flowing into climate, conservation and forest governance, do we necessarily need more financial mechanisms—or do we need to think differently about how existing finance is organised and governed?

This is where I want to begin thinking from the perspective of landscape governance.
