# When Certification Meets Multiple Rules

## Reflections from a Cocoa Stakeholder Meeting

**Reflection · October 2026**  
*Cocoa certification · Child labour · Legal pluralism · Environmental governance*

### Reflections from a Rainforest Alliance stakeholder meeting

Yesterday, I participated in a Rainforest Alliance stakeholder meeting discussing its recent module on child labour and developments in its cocoa certification process. The participants came from different parts of the cocoa value chain, including traders, buyers, supply-chain actors, certification bodies, and independent stakeholders such as myself.

I sat at a table with representatives from Ferrero, Callebaut, and Cargill, as well as certification organisations. At first, I wondered how the participation of such large companies would shape the discussion. What struck me, however, was how actively they contributed—not simply as observers, but by drawing on their practical knowledge of agricultural trade, certification and farming practices in the regions where they operate.

Much of the discussion focused on Latin America—particularly Ecuador—and West Africa, especially Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire, with some reference to Indonesia. It was interesting to hear how differently cocoa production and certification challenges are experienced across these regions.

In parts of Latin America, farmers were described as having relatively stronger market positions and greater access to certification and commercial opportunities. Some farmers compete in more open markets and can generate comparatively higher incomes. In this context, issues such as child labour appear to be less prominent in the certification discussions.

The situation described for Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire was quite different. Despite their importance to global cocoa production, many smallholder farmers continue to operate under significant economic constraints. Certification can also intersect with local systems of land and family organisation, including traditions around the division and inheritance of farm plots. Child labour presents another complex issue. Children may participate in family farming activities, which raises questions about where the boundary lies between family assistance, agricultural socialisation and forms of work that are prohibited because they can harm children—for example, exposure to agricultural chemicals.

One part of the discussion particularly caught my attention: which rules should apply when implementing child-labour requirements. Rainforest Alliance has its own certification requirements, while each producing country has its own laws and regulatory frameworks concerning child labour. This creates a practical question that is broader than cocoa certification: **how do private standards interact with existing public rules and locally embedded practices?**

This immediately brought me back to my research on **legal pluralism**.

From this perspective, the challenge may not necessarily be to create the most detailed set of private rules possible. Rather, there may be value in recognising the different legal and normative orders that already operate in a particular context, and considering from the outset how a certification system can accommodate, complement or align with them.

This does not mean that private standards should simply defer to existing regulations. International standards may provide important safeguards where national regulation is weak or poorly enforced. But engaging explicitly with existing formal regulations—and, where relevant, the local institutions and practices through which those regulations are interpreted and implemented—could potentially reduce confusion and make certification requirements more workable.

For me, this was one of the most interesting aspects of the meeting. **Certification is not implemented in an institutional vacuum.** It enters landscapes where government regulations, private standards, market requirements and local practices already coexist. Understanding how these different systems interact may therefore be just as important as defining the standard itself.

It was also a useful reminder of why I find governance research so interesting: sometimes a discussion about a very practical issue—such as a child-labour module in a cocoa certification system—opens up much broader questions about institutions, authority and how different sets of rules can work together in practice.

"Why this matters"
This reflection connects to my broader interest in how multiple systems of rules and authority interact in practice. When international standards meet national regulations and locally embedded institutions.
