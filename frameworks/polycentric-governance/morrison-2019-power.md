# 🌿 Morrison et al. (2019): The Black Box of Power in Polycentric Environmental Governance

## Why this paper matters

Much of the literature on polycentric governance highlights the benefits of multiple decision-making centres, including learning, flexibility, and adaptation.

Morrison et al argue that an important dimension has often been overlooked: **power**.

This paper challenges researchers to move beyond describing governance structures and to examine how power shapes interactions, participation, and outcomes within polycentric systems.

---

## The central question

How does power influence the functioning of polycentric environmental governance?

---

## Core contribution

The authors argue that power should not be treated as a background condition but as a central feature of polycentric governance.

Power influences:

- who participates
- who makes decisions
- whose knowledge is recognised
- whose interests are prioritised
- how conflicts are resolved

Understanding these dynamics is essential for explaining why some polycentric systems succeed while others struggle.

---

## Key ideas I take from this paper

### Polycentric governance is not power-neutral

Multiple centres of governance do not automatically imply equal influence.

### Power shapes coordination

Coordination depends not only on institutional arrangements but also on relationships of authority, legitimacy, and resources.

### Governance outcomes reflect unequal influence

Differences in political, legal, financial, and social power shape environmental decision-making.

## How I use this paper in my research

This paper helps me move beyond identifying multiple governance actors.
